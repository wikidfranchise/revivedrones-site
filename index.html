The Problem

The busiest new airspace in America has no referee.

Below 500 feet, air ambulances, law-enforcement helicopters, commercial delivery drones, and recreational aircraft share the same sky with no surveillance, no separation service, and no way to give a medevac priority over a package. Radar doesn't reach down here. Traditional ATC was never built for it.

The FAA's pending Part 108 rulemaking — the largest in modern agency history by public response — will require exactly the capabilities that don't yet exist at scale: electronic conspicuity, automated deconfliction, and authoritative right-of-way.

The System

A neutral registry, run the way payment networks are run.

The Pecking Order registers every cooperative aircraft in the managed airspace, gives each an identity and a priority, and deconflicts their routes before they ever launch. Life-critical operations — air ambulances, law enforcement, disaster response — automatically outrank commercial and recreational traffic.

Authoritative

One registry of record for identity, priority, and right-of-way in the managed sector — the enforcement layer the new rules contemplate.

Neutral

Like the payment networks, the registry operates no aircraft and competes with no operator. It authenticates, arbitrates, and records — nothing else.

Resilient

Satellite-native by design, so it works where terrestrial networks don't: rural, wilderness, tribal, and disaster-affected areas that need low-altitude aviation most.

The Founder

Built by an architect who hit the wall himself.

Revive Drones began as a drone venture — and its founder, a retired solutions architect with multiple issued U.S. patents in communications and data management, ran into what every operator below 500 feet runs into: nobody is managing anything. Eighteen months of design and engineering later, the system that should have existed does.
